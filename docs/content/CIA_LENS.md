# The CIA lens, verified against the opened Standards (19 Nov 2026)

How The IIA's exams think, line by line, each with the Standard that supports
it. This is the long form behind the short lens in `docs/content/CIA_BLUEPRINT.md`
and the item standard in `.claude/agents/cia-standards-auditor.md`. Change it
only after re-opening the Standards, and record the change in
`docs/content/CIA_RESEARCH_LOG.md`.

Source read: Global Internal Audit Standards, published 9 January 2024 (opened 19 Nov 2026: 120 pages, 5 domains, 15
principles, 52 standards). The 2017-to-2024 two-way mapping was read only to see what changed.
Everything below is in KaiSip's own words. Each line ends with the Standard
that supports it. "(R)" means the Requirements part of a standard, "(EC)" the
Essential Conditions for the board and senior management, "(CI)" the
Considerations for Implementation, which use "should" and "may" and are not
mandatory. A key that turns on a "(CI)" line must say "should" or "leading
practice", never "must".

KaiSip is independent and is not affiliated with The IIA. No prep provider's
bank was opened.

## The verified lens

### 1. Purpose, and who the internal auditor serves
- Internal auditing makes the organization better able to create value, protect it and sustain it. It does this by supplying the board and management with assurance, advice, insight and foresight that are independent, objective and based on risk. [Domain I, Purpose Statement]
- It works best when three things hold: competent people do the work and conform with the Standards, the function sits in an independent position and answers straight to the board, and no one exerts undue influence over the internal auditors. [Domain I, Purpose Statement]
- The chief audit executive answers for the whole function's conformance; every internal auditor answers for the ethics and engagement standards that touch their own work. [Fundamentals, "Applicability and Elements of the Standards"; Domains II and V]

### 2. Independence belongs to the function, objectivity to the person
- Independence means the internal audit function is free of anything that would weaken its ability to work without bias. Objectivity is the unbiased mindset of each internal auditor. [Glossary; Principle 7; Principle 2; Standard 2.1 Individual Objectivity]
- Independence comes from position: the chief audit executive's direct reporting line runs to the board (the Standards also call this functional reporting), and there is usually an administrative line to a member of management. Making the chief executive officer the administrative line is a leading practice, not a requirement. [Standard 7.1 Organizational Independence (EC, CI); Domain IV introduction]
- The chief audit executive confirms the function's independence to the board at least once a year, and tells the board about incidents that may have impaired it and the safeguards used. [Standard 7.1 (R)]
- Roles that could impair independence are discussed with the board and senior management, and the chief audit executive advises both on safeguards. [Standard 7.1 (R)]
- Remedy for an objectivity problem: the internal auditor discloses it to a designated supervisor or to the chief audit executive. If it affects the work, the chief audit executive talks it over with one or more of three parties (the board, senior management, the management of the activity under review) and decides the action (options named in the considerations: reassign, reschedule, adjust the scope, outsource the performance or supervision). [Standard 2.3 Disclosing Impairments to Objectivity (R, CI)]
- An impairment of the chief audit executive's own objectivity, whether real or only apparent, is disclosed to the board. [Standard 2.3 (R)]
- Hard rules on objectivity: an internal auditor must not assess a specific activity they used to be responsible for (the requirement has no time limit), and for assurance work objectivity is presumed impaired when that responsibility ended within the previous 12 months; assurance after advisory work is allowed only when the chief audit executive confirms the advisory work does not impair objectivity and staffs the engagement to manage it; advisory work on an area of previous responsibility is allowed after disclosing the possible impairment to the requesting party before accepting; no accepting a gift, favor, reward or similar item when it could impair objectivity or could be presumed to. [Standard 2.2 Safeguarding Objectivity (R)]
- Perceived impairments count, not only actual ones. [Standards 2.2 and 2.3 (R); Standard 7.1 (R)]

### 3. When the chief audit executive has roles beyond internal auditing
- An ongoing role: the responsibilities, the nature of the work and the safeguards are written into the internal audit charter, and if those areas are subject to internal auditing, assurance comes another way, for example from an outside assurance provider that is competent and objective and reports to the board independently. [Standard 7.1 (R)]
- A temporary role: an independent third party provides assurance over those areas during the assignment and for the following 12 months, and the chief audit executive sets a plan to hand the responsibilities back to management. [Standard 7.1 (R)]
- When the chief audit executive is responsible for a function, any assurance engagement over it is overseen by an independent party that sits outside the internal audit function. [Standard 2.2 (R)]
- The board acknowledges the impairment when it approves such roles, and it works out the safeguards together with the chief audit executive and senior management. [Standard 7.1 (EC)]

### 4. Who approves what
- The chief audit executive gives the board and senior management the information they need to set the internal audit mandate (authority, role and responsibilities). The mandate may come from the board, from laws and regulations, or both. [Standard 6.1 Internal Audit Mandate (R); Glossary "internal audit mandate"; Principle 6]
- The chief audit executive develops and maintains the internal audit charter and discusses the draft with the board and senior management. The board approves it. Senior management gives input; it does not approve. [Standard 6.2 Internal Audit Charter (R, EC); Standard 6.1 (R, EC)]
- The charter states at least: the Purpose of Internal Auditing, the commitment to follow the Standards, the mandate (covering which services are provided and how far they reach, and what the board is responsible for and expects by way of management's support), and the organizational position and reporting relationships. [Standard 6.2 (R)]
- The chief audit executive creates the internal audit plan and discusses it with the board and senior management, including any significant change made during the period. Approval of the plan, and of each significant change to it, belongs to the board. [Standard 9.4 Internal Audit Plan (R)]
- The chief audit executive develops the budget and takes it to the board for approval. The board's essential conditions list approving the charter, the plan, the budget and the resource plan. [Standard 10.1 Financial Resource Management (R); Standard 6.3 Board and Senior Management Support (EC)]
- Resources: the chief audit executive evaluates whether they are sufficient and, if not, sets a strategy to obtain them and tells the board the impact. The board works with senior management to provide sufficient resources. [Standard 8.2 Resources (R, EC)]
- Appointing or removing the chief audit executive needs the board's authorization. On performance evaluation and pay it gives input to senior management; the Standards do not say the board sets or approves the pay. [Standard 7.1 (EC)]
- The board approves the function's performance objectives at least once a year, and reviews and approves the chief audit executive's plan for the external quality assessment. [Standard 8.3 Quality (EC); Standard 8.4 External Quality Assessment (EC)]
- The internal audit strategy (vision, strategic objectives, supporting initiatives) is reviewed with the board and senior management periodically. The Standards require review, not approval. [Standard 9.2 Internal Audit Strategy (R)]
- "Board" means the highest-level governing body, which can be an audit committee. [Glossary "board"]
- The board and senior management lines above are essential conditions, not requirements placed on the board. The chief audit executive's duty is to discuss them, show with examples what their absence does, and document any agreed alternative or the disagreement. [Domain III introduction, "Disagreements on Essential Conditions"]

### 5. Management owns risk, controls and actions
- A control is anything that management, the board or another party does to manage risk. Advisory services give advice without assurance and without taking on management responsibilities. [Glossary "control", "advisory services"; Domain V introduction]
- Internal auditors decide, finding by finding, whether to develop recommendations, ask management for action plans, or settle the actions jointly with management. Carrying the actions out is management's responsibility. [Standard 14.4 Recommendations and Action Plans (R, CI)]
- Internal auditors confirm that management has implemented the recommendations or action plans; they do not implement them. [Standard 15.2 Confirming the Implementation of Recommendations or Action Plans (R)]
- The Standards apply to assurance and advisory work alike, except where a standard says otherwise (a formal risk assessment, evaluation criteria and evidence-based findings may not be needed for advisory work, depending on the agreement). [Domain V introduction; Standards 13.2, 13.4 and 14.2 (R)]
- In an assurance engagement it is mainly the internal auditors who decide the objectives and scope; in an advisory engagement the auditors and the activity's management typically decide them together. [Standard 13.3 Engagement Objectives and Scope (CI)]

### 6. When management accepts too much risk
- The trigger is the chief audit executive's conclusion that the level of risk management has accepted is above the organization's risk appetite or risk tolerance. It is not the chief audit executive's personal preference. [Standard 11.5 Communicating the Acceptance of Risks (R)]
- Sequence: first try to settle it with the managers in charge of that risk area (CI); then discuss it with senior management (R); if the chief audit executive determines senior management has not resolved it, escalate to the board (R). [Standard 11.5 (R, CI)]
- Resolving the risk is not the chief audit executive's responsibility. How the concern is taken up with management is for the board to decide. [Standard 11.5 (R, CI)]
- Delay counts: when action plans slip, the internal auditor gets and documents management's explanation and takes it to the chief audit executive, who determines whether the delay or lack of action means senior management has accepted a risk beyond the risk tolerance. [Standard 15.2 (R)]
- The board sets, with the chief audit executive, the criteria and process for what is escalated to it. [Standard 8.1 Board Interaction (EC)]

### 7. Risk decides the plan and the engagement
- The plan rests on a documented assessment, done at least annually, that covers the organization's risks, objectives and strategies and takes input from the board and senior management. The plan is dynamic and updated in time. [Standard 9.4 (R)]
- The chief audit executive tells the board and senior management, in time, about the impact of resource limits on coverage, the reason the plan gives a high-risk area no assurance engagement, conflicting demands for services, and limits on scope or access. [Standard 9.4 (R)]
- Each engagement starts with understanding the activity under review and an engagement risk assessment, then objectives and scope, evaluation criteria, resources and a work program. [Principle 13; Standards 13.2 to 13.6 (R)]
- Each engagement's objectives and scope, and any later change to them, need the chief audit executive's approval. The chief audit executive also reviews and approves the work program before it is carried out and promptly after changes. These duties may be delegated to an engagement supervisor; accountability stays with the chief audit executive. [Standard 13.3 (R); Standard 13.6 Work Program (R); Glossary "engagement supervisor"; Domain V introduction]
- Planning comes before testing, but the steps overlap and repeat: the risk assessment and the work program may change as work goes on. [Domain V introduction; Standard 14.2 (R)]
- A scope limitation is discussed with management to resolve it; if it cannot be resolved, the chief audit executive elevates it to the board. [Standard 13.3 (R)]
- Evaluation criteria: use the criteria the board and senior management have set if they are adequate; if not, find appropriate criteria by talking with senior management and/or the board. [Standard 13.4 Evaluation Criteria (R)]
- A Topical Requirement must be followed when an engagement's scope includes that topic. Assurance services must conform with it; for advisory services conformance is recommended. It applies in three cases: an engagement in the plan is about the topic, the topic turns up during an engagement, or someone asks for an engagement on it that was not in the original plan. Each requirement is assessed for applicability, and any exclusion is documented with its reason. [Standards, "About the International Professional Practices Framework"; Cybersecurity Topical Requirement, opening page, opened 19 Nov 2026]

### 8. Evidence and findings
- Information must be relevant, reliable and sufficient (three attributes, in that order). [Standard 14.1 Gathering Information for Analyses and Evaluation (R)]
- Information is more reliable when the internal auditor gets it first-hand or an independent source supplies it, when it is corroborated, and when the system it comes from has effective governance, risk management and control processes. [Standard 14.1 (R)]
- Sufficient means there is enough for someone prudent, informed and competent to redo the work and reach the same conclusions. Documentation is held to the same test. [Standard 14.1 (R); Standard 14.6 Engagement Documentation (R)]
- When relevant evidence is not obtainable, the internal auditor decides whether that is itself a finding. [Standard 14.1 (R)]
- A finding is the gap that exists when the condition (the existing state) does not match the evaluation criteria. [Glossary "finding"; Standard 14.2 Analyses and Potential Engagement Findings (R)]
- Each potential finding is then evaluated: the root cause (with management, when possible), the potential effect, and the significance, judged by likelihood and impact. [Standard 14.3 Evaluation of Findings (R)]
- A significant risk must be documented as a finding and communicated as one; other risks are reported according to the circumstances and the function's methodologies. Findings are prioritized by significance. [Standard 14.3 (R)]
- Actions should fix the root cause, bring the risk to an acceptable level and close the gap. [Standard 14.4 (R)]
- When no gap is found, the conclusion still gives assurance that the processes are effective. [Standard 14.2 (R); Standard 14.5 Engagement Conclusions (R)]

### 9. Disagreements, final communication and monitoring
- If management disagrees with the engagement results, the internal auditors talk the issue through with management and aim for a shared understanding. They need not change the results without a valid reason, and a set methodology lets both sides state their positions. [Standard 13.1 Engagement Communication (R); Standard 14.4 (R)]
- The final communication gives the objectives, scope, conclusions and, if applicable, recommendations and/or action plans. For assurance engagements it adds the findings with their significance and priority, any scope limitation, and a conclusion on the effectiveness of governance, risk management and control processes. It names who will act and by when, and it acknowledges actions management has already started or finished. [Standard 15.1 Final Engagement Communication (R)]
- The chief audit executive reviews and approves it before issue and decides who receives it. Before release outside the organization, the chief audit executive gets advice, as required, from legal counsel, senior management or both. [Standard 15.1 (R); Standard 11.3 Communicating Results (R)]
- A significant error or omission: corrected information goes promptly to everyone who received the original; "significant" follows criteria agreed with the board. [Standard 11.4 Errors and Omissions (R)]
- Follow-up: inquire about progress, do follow-up assessments on a risk basis, and keep a tracking system up to date with the status. How far these steps go depends on the finding's significance. [Standard 15.2 (R)]
- Themes across engagements, and any conclusion at the level of a business unit or the organization, go to the board and senior management. [Standard 11.3 (R)]

### 10. Fraud
- Every engagement risk assessment considers specific risks related to fraud. (Advisory work may not need a risk assessment that is formal and documented.) [Standard 13.2 Engagement Risk Assessment (R)]
- Due professional care includes weighing how likely significant errors, fraud and noncompliance are. [Standard 4.2 Due Professional Care (R)]
- The internal audit plan considers coverage of fraud risk. [Standard 9.4 (R)]
- Behavior against the organization's ethical expectations is reported under the applicable policies and procedures; legal or regulatory violations are reported to people or bodies with authority to act, as laws, regulations and policies specify. Findings are never hidden or left out. [Standard 1.2 Organization's Ethical Expectations (R); Standard 1.3 Legal and Ethical Behavior (R); Standard 1.1 Honesty and Professional Courage (R)]
- Internal auditors take on only work they have, or can obtain, the competencies for. Forensic work is listed as an example of advisory services, and investigations may be among the services the mandate names. [Standard 3.1 Competency (R); Glossary "advisory services"; Standard 6.1 (CI)]
- The Standards say nothing more specific about red flags, the fraud triangle or how to investigate. The Part 1 syllabus, an opened IIA document, names the three fraud triangle conditions; a key may rest on exactly that. Anything beyond it rests on The IIA's practice guides and the textbook, which must be opened before an item is keyed on them.

### 11. Quality and conformance
- The chief audit executive runs a quality assurance and improvement program covering the whole function, with internal and external assessments. [Standard 8.3 (R); Principle 12]
- Internal assessments are ongoing monitoring plus periodic assessments, done as self-assessments or by others in the organization who know internal audit practice well enough. Results go to the board and senior management at least annually. [Standard 12.1 Internal Quality Assessment (R); Standard 8.3 (R)]
- At least once in every five years the function has an external assessment, done by an assessor or assessment team that is independent and qualified. A self-assessment with independent validation also meets the requirement. At least one person on the assessing side must be a Certified Internal Auditor in active status. Results are reported when completed. [Standard 8.4 (R); Standard 8.3 (R)]
- The chief audit executive plans the external assessment and discusses the plan with the board (R). The board reviews and approves the plan and receives the complete results directly from the assessor (EC). [Standard 8.4 (R, EC)]
- Not independent for this purpose: people from another department of the same organization, people from a related organization, and two organizations reviewing each other. Assessments that rotate around a group of at least three peer organizations may be. [Standard 8.4 (CI)]
- When a requirement cannot be met, the chief audit executive documents and communicates the circumstance, the alternative actions, their impact and the reason. If a law prevents conformance with part of the Standards, the rest still applies and the fact is disclosed. [Standard 4.1 Conformance with the Global Internal Audit Standards (R)]
- Nonconformance that affects the function's scope or operation as a whole is disclosed, with its impact, to the board and senior management. [Standard 12.1 (R)]
- An engagement not done in conformance: the final communication names the standard or standards, the reason and the impact on the findings and conclusions. [Standard 15.1 (R)]
- A conformance statement for an engagement is appropriate only when both engagement supervision and the quality assurance and improvement program have produced results that support it. This is a consideration ("should"), not a requirement. [Standard 15.1 (CI)]
- The chief audit executive sets performance objectives with input from the board and senior management and measures progress. [Standard 12.2 Performance Measurement (R)]

### 12. The seat of each exam (KaiSip's teaching frame, checked against the Standards and the syllabuses)
- Part 1: an internal auditor who knows the rules and who does what. Several Part 1 topics are about the board's and the chief audit executive's duties; the candidate answers as someone who knows them, not as the person who performs them. [Fundamentals; Domains I to III; Part 1 syllabus]
- Part 2: the internal auditor running an engagement, under an engagement supervisor. Approvals in the engagement (objectives and scope, work program, documentation, final communication) belong to the chief audit executive or a delegate. [Principles 13 and 14; Standard 12.3 Oversee and Improve Engagement Performance (R); Part 2 syllabus]
- Part 3: the chief audit executive running the function (sections A to C) and approving, sending and escalating results (section D). Inside section D, drafting the final communication and confirming action plans is still internal auditors' work. [Domain IV; Standards 15.1 and 15.2 (R); Part 3 syllabus]
- Challenge Exam: The IIA says it tests practical application at an advanced level for experienced or already-credentialed people. Its 40 topic lines match Part lines, so the seat follows the matching Part. [The IIA's Challenge Exam pages, opened 19 Nov 2026; Challenge syllabus]

## Corrections to the starting lens

1. Risk acceptance test. The starting lens said "a risk the chief audit executive believes is beyond what the organization should accept". Standard 11.5 keys on a risk above the organization's risk appetite or risk tolerance. (Topic 3D6's syllabus line still uses the looser phrase "may be unacceptable to the organization", which is the 2017 wording.)
2. Risk acceptance sequence. Add the step before senior management: the chief audit executive should first raise it with the managers in charge of the risk (Standard 11.5 CI). Add what happens after: the board decides how to address it (CI), and "not the chief audit executive's responsibility to resolve" (R). Add the link from late action plans (Standard 15.2).
3. "The board approves the mandate, the charter, the plan and the resources." More exact: the board approves the charter (which contains the mandate), the plan and any significant change to it, the budget and the resource plan (Standards 6.2, 9.4, 10.1; Standard 6.3 EC). Sufficient resources are provided by the board together with senior management (Standard 8.2 EC). The mandate can also come from law (Glossary, Principle 6).
4. Senior management does not approve the charter or the plan. The 2017 text sent the plan to "senior management and the board" for approval, while final approval of the charter already rested with the board; the 2024 text has discussion with both and approval by the board only, for the charter and the plan alike (Standards 6.2 and 9.4; the mapping rows for the old charter and plan standards).
5. Board duties are "essential conditions", not requirements on the board (Domain III introduction). The chief audit executive's requirement is to discuss, explain the impact and document. The starting lens did not have this.
6. "It reports to the board through the chief audit executive." More exact: the chief audit executive's direct line is to the board, with an administrative line to management; chief executive officer reporting is a leading practice in the considerations (Standard 7.1). Added: annual confirmation of independence to the board (Standard 7.1 R).
7. Chief audit executive pay. The board gives senior management input on the chief audit executive's remuneration and performance evaluation; it authorizes appointment and removal (Standard 7.1 EC). Do not key "the board sets the pay".
8. "Never takes a management decision or owns a fix" is right for an internal auditor in an engagement, but the Standards allow the chief audit executive to hold roles beyond internal auditing with safeguards (Standard 7.1 R; Standard 2.2 R). Added section 3.
9. "Recommends" is one of three choices: develop recommendations, request action plans, or agree actions together (Standard 14.4 R).
10. Evidence order and count: "relevant, reliable, sufficient", three attributes (Standard 14.1 R). The 2017 text had four (it added "useful").
11. Findings: root cause is identified with management "when possible" (Standard 14.3 R); significance uses likelihood and impact; a significant risk must be reported as a finding.
12. Fraud. "Assess fraud risk in every engagement" is supported (Standard 13.2 R), with the advisory exception. "Report red flags through the right channel" is supported only in general terms (Standards 1.1 to 1.3). "Investigation needs the right skills and authority" has no standard of its own: the nearest text is Standard 3.1 on competency, the glossary's "forensic services" example and Standard 6.1 CI. The 2017 sentence that internal auditors need not have an investigator's expertise is no longer a requirement (the mapping sends it to a Standard 3.1 consideration).
13. External quality assessment. Added: at least every five years; a qualified, independent assessor or team; self-assessment with independent validation is an accepted route; at least one active Certified Internal Auditor on the assessing side (new in 2024); the board approves the plan and receives the results directly (Standard 8.4).
14. Conformance statement. It may be used only when engagement supervision and the quality program support it, and that sentence sits in the considerations (Standard 15.1 CI). The Standard says "in conformance with"; syllabus topic 3D2 says "in accordance with".
15. Seat of Part 3. Section D mixes seats: internal auditors draft the final communication and confirm action plans (Standards 15.1, 15.2); the chief audit executive approves, disseminates, judges risk acceptance and escalates.
16. Strategy. The Standards require the chief audit executive to review the internal audit strategy periodically with the board and senior management; they do not require board approval (Standard 9.2 R).

## Where the Standards text was ambiguous or silent

- Topic 3D6 wording ("may be unacceptable to the organization") against Standard 11.5 (above the organization's "risk appetite or risk tolerance"). KaiSip keys on Standard 11.5 and treats the syllabus phrase as the topic label.
- Standard 11.5 (R) starts at senior management; only the considerations add "first the responsible management". An item must not key "discuss with the responsible manager first" as a requirement, and must not make "senior management" and "the responsible manager" compete as the final two unless the stem settles which stage has passed.
- Standard 11.5 says "risk appetite or risk tolerance"; Standard 15.2 says only "risk tolerance". Do not write an item that turns on the difference.
- Standard 8.4 ties the rule that at least one assessor must be a Certified Internal Auditor in active status to the step of choosing the independent assessor or team. For a self-assessment with independent validation the text does not say separately whether the validator must be that person. Do not key it.
- Standard 13.6 and Standard 14.6 say the chief audit executive "must" approve the work program and the engagement documentation; the glossary and the Domain V introduction allow delegation to an engagement supervisor. Stems must say who holds the delegated role.
- Standard 9.5 sends coordination concerns to senior management first "and, if necessary, the board": the order is given, the trigger for the board is not.
- "Limited" versus "reasonable" assurance appears once, in the glossary, with no definition of either. Topic 1A4 needs another opened source for more than "it depends on which procedures are done, when, and how extensively".
- The Three Lines Model is not in the Standards. Its source is The IIA's Statement of Position "Three Lines Model: Assurance and Advice in Support of Effective Governance" (2026, opened 19 Nov 2026), which replaced the 2020 position paper. In short, in our words: management in first-line roles owns and manages risk and designs and runs controls; second-line roles give specialist support, monitoring and challenge; the third line is the internal audit function, which gives independent assurance (including over the second line) and advice without management responsibility, and reports functionally to the board.
- The fraud triangle and the control types are not in the Standards (only a one-line list of four types in a Standard 13.2 consideration). The Part 1 syllabus, an opened IIA document, names the three fraud triangle conditions (1D1a) and classes authority levels as preventing fraud and hotlines, reconciliations and supervisory reviews as detecting it (1D4c, 1D4d). A key may rest on exactly those syllabus classifications, cited first. Anything beyond them (definitions, whether all three sides must be present, directive or corrective, compensating controls) still waits for a practice guide or the textbook. Internal audit mission statements and the IT, finance and business-process knowledge of topic 2A3 are not in the Standards either.
- "IPPF" is still the current framework's name in the 2024 Standards (it holds the Standards, the Topical Requirements and Global Guidance). Only its 2017 structure and element names are stale, so the stale-terms gate should not ban the bare abbreviation.
- The 2024 glossary entry for advisory services adds "also known as consulting services", and "Code of Ethics" appears as the "former Code of Ethics". Both old terms are confirmed as old by the opened text. "Three Lines of Defense" is confirmed as the old name by the title of the 2020 position paper (opened). The glossary comparison (opened) confirms "engagement opinion" became "engagement conclusion" and "overall opinion" became "results of internal audit services". "Implementation Guidance", "Practice Advisory" and the 2019 part and domain names do not appear in any opened file, so their status as old terms is not yet confirmed from an opened source.
